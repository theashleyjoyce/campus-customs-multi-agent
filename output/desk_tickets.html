Reflection

Written after the run on the Ticket tabs and the Cash tab. Every number here comes off those tabs or out of data/campus_customs_new.db.

1. How did the agents actually perform on each ticket?

Ticket 101, Bulldog tee — right answer, slow route

This is the one I would grade highest on judgement and lowest on efficiency. The team found the thing that actually mattered, which was that the tee being at 0 was not the real problem. The real problem was that Bulldog Print Co would not ship anything while invoice 501 sat open at $840 and three days overdue. Inventory worked that out and went straight to Accounting about it, which is the behaviour I wanted.

It cost 65 turns across four agents and three passes of the board. It also needed two separate human approvals, the $840 invoice and then an $8 purchase order for the tee itself, which you can see on the Ticket 101 tab. The second one I had not planned for at all.

The thing I am most pleased about is what it did not do. It never promised Tauhid a delivery date. There was no honest one to give, and the Customer Service draft says so.

Ticket 102, rent — correct, and badly over-staffed

Facilities did the one thing that mattered. It did not take the landlord's email at face value. It pulled lease 1 and confirmed $2,400 due 2026-09-02, which is two days after the shop date of 2026-08-31, so not late. That is the whole job.

It then took 37 turns and four agents to do it. Customer Service drafted a reply to Elm City Properties, who sent us a notice and were not waiting on an answer. This ticket is the weakest argument for a team in the whole run.

Ticket 103, bulk hoodies — the best decision making

This one resolved having spent $0.00, which the Cash tab confirms, and I think that is the right outcome rather than a failure.

Accounting used quote_discount instead of doing percentages in its head, and landed on 20 percent off: $46.40 a unit against a $22 cost, $928 on the order, $488 of margin kept. The break-even on CC-HOOD-NAVY is 62.1 percent, so 20 percent is nowhere near the line. Then it worked out that restocking the 12 missing hoodies at $264 did not fit against what was left, and closed the ticket saying so plainly instead of pretending.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Ticket 102 should have been one agent. The whole job is read lease 1, compare $2,400 and 2026-09-02 against the shop date, check checking has $3,400, and raise an approval. That is three tool calls: get_lease, get_cash_balance, request_approval. One agent with those three does it in about four turns. We spent 37 and pulled in a Customer Service draft nobody asked for. Nothing about rent needs specialist knowledge, because the lease row already contains the answer.

The discount half of 103 too. get_pricing and quote_discount on CC-HOOD-NAVY is arithmetic. The tool already returns the break-even of 62.1 percent. There is no judgement to split across agents.

Where the team genuinely earned it: ticket 101. Stock and money are actually different problems with different data behind them. Inventory asked check_vendor_for_restock and got back can_ship: false with invoice 501 named. It then handed that to Accounting, who owns cash. A single agent could have done it with the same 17 tools, but the hand-off is what surfaced the chokepoint as a money problem rather than a stock problem, and that reframing is the whole answer to the ticket.

My honest read is that on a board this small, one agent with all 17 tools would have resolved all three tickets faster and cheaper. The team pays off when a question crosses domains, which was one ticket out of three.

4. New problems this team could already solve

These need no new tools and no new agents.

The Crest Mug is at zero and nobody has noticed. CC-MUG-CREST in OS is sitting at 0 in inventory, same as the tee was. When someone orders one, it runs the exact ticket 101 play, except it routes to Elm City Gifts, whose specialty is mugs and small goods and who have a 3 day lead. They hold no open invoice, so check_vendor_for_restock returns can_ship: true and there is no chokepoint to clear. Faster than the tee was.

Thin sizes before a rush. CC-TEE-WHITE is down to 2 in XL and 3 in L, and CC-HOOD-NAVY has 4 left in S. A "restock before move-in weekend" ticket is just check_stock across sizes, then check_vendor_for_restock, then one approval per order. The team already does every step of that.

A discount on a product we actually have. CC-HAT-BLUE has 40 in stock at $6 cost and $22 list, break-even 72.7 percent. A bulk request from another student org is ticket 103 without the hard part, because no restock is needed. Accounting answers it alone with quote_discount.

A second frozen vendor. If Elm City Gifts ever goes overdue, mugs and small goods freeze exactly the way apparel did. The team has already proved it can find that pattern, because the whole of ticket 101 was finding it.

5. Three problems this team cannot solve, and what it would take

One. Tauhid returns the tee and wants his money back

Cash only goes out in this shop. pay_bill subtracts from checking and there is no tool anywhere that adds to it. There is also no table recording that a sale happened, so there is nothing to return against. If Tauhid walks back in with the tee, the team can draft him a nice message and do absolutely nothing else.

What we would need: a sales table so an order exists to refund, a returns table, and two tools, record_return(sku, size, qty, reason, sale_id) which puts the unit back on the shelf, and refund_customer(sale_id, amount, approved_by) which moves cash the other way under the same human approval rule. The balance guard also has to learn that money can arrive, which it currently assumes never happens.

Two. "When is my tee actually getting here?"

We paid $8 for the tee restock on ticket 101, and nothing in the database records that an order exists. There is a payment row and an approval marked paid, and that is all. No expected arrival, no order number, no link to Bulldog Print Co's 5 day lead. This is not hypothetical: it is exactly why ticket 101 refused to close twice. The boss kept saying the purchase order was not recorded or confirmed, and it was right.

What we would need: a purchase_orders table with vendor, sku, size, qty, ordered date and an expected arrival computed from vendors.lead_days. Then place_order, list_open_orders, and a receive_stock that takes an order id instead of a bare sku so arrivals reconcile against what was ordered. A Logistics agent would own that, and Customer Service could finally quote a real date instead of being told not to promise one.

Three. A new product arrives and nobody knows who makes it

Say the shop adds CC-SCARF-NAVY. There is no column anywhere linking a sku to a vendor. The only signal on file is the free text in vendors.specialty, which is why check_vendor_for_restock deliberately refuses to guess and hands back all three vendors for the agent to choose from. With four skus and three vendors a human can eyeball it. With forty skus it is a guessing game, and "apparel reprint" does not obviously cover a scarf.

What we would need: a sku_vendor table holding sku, vendor id, unit cost from that vendor, minimum order quantity and any lead time that differs from the vendor default. Then find_supplier(sku) reads a real relationship instead of inferring one, and it can return more than one supplier so Accounting can compare prices. A Sourcing agent would own vendor relationships, and Inventory would stop making a call it has no data for.

All three have the same shape. The team is good at reasoning over what the database knows. Every one of these fails because the database does not know something, and no amount of agent cleverness fixes a missing table.
